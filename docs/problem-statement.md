# Problem Statement

## Background

Environmental education in Indian schools and higher-education institutions is largely
theoretical and textbook-driven. Despite NEP 2020's emphasis on sustainability and
experiential learning, students encounter environmental topics as content to be memorised
rather than as challenges to act on. The result is a persistent gap between awareness and
behaviour — students can name the causes of climate change or water scarcity but have no
structured mechanism to practise sustainable habits, record that practice, or see how their
individual choices connect to a wider community effort.

## The Problem

Students lack an engaging, verifiable path from learning an environmental concept to
completing a real-world action tied to that concept and receiving meaningful feedback on it.
Today's classroom tools deliver passive reading or one-off quiz exercises; they do not close
the loop between knowledge and action. A student who finishes a lesson on waste management
has no guided next step, no way to document that they actually separated dry and wet waste
at home, and no feedback that distinguishes their genuine effort from an unrelated photo
upload. Teachers face the mirror problem: they can see quiz scores but have no concise,
prioritised view of which topics their class is struggling with and what to do about it next.
Organizers coordinating multi-school environmental programmes have no shared platform to run
and track inter-school competitions at scale.

## Who Is Affected

Three distinct roles inside Indian schools and educational institutions experience this
problem directly. **Students** — the primary users — need personalised guidance on what to
learn next, missions that translate topics into observable real-world actions (tree
plantation, waste segregation, water conservation, clean campus activity, and green
transport), and a feedback loop that tells them whether their evidence photo actually shows
what the mission required. **Teachers** manage a class of students whose progress spans
multiple topics and missions; without an aggregated, AI-generated action list they must
manually review every submission and infer patterns themselves. **Organizers** coordinate
programmes across schools and need a competition management layer that is absent from any
individual school's existing tools.

## Why It Matters Now

NEP 2020 explicitly calls for sustainability and experiential learning to be embedded in
curricula, yet the tooling to operationalise that intent does not exist at the classroom
level. Without a closed learn-to-act loop, environmental education remains awareness theatre
rather than behaviour change. The problem is urgent because the habits students form now
determine long-term environmental outcomes, and the window to embed those habits during
formal education is narrow. Passive reading alone does not build lasting sustainable
practice.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

General educational platforms (quizzes, digital textbooks, LMS modules) address knowledge
delivery but stop there. They have no concept of a real-world mission, no evidence
submission and verification step, and no AI layer that can distinguish a genuine tree-
planting photo from an unrelated upload. Gamification platforms exist but are domain-
agnostic and do not tie point-earning to verifiable environmental actions. Teachers using
spreadsheets or dashboard tools can view individual scores but receive no prioritised
guidance on what to address for the class as a whole. None of these tools support the
student-teacher-organizer triad that a school-level environmental programme requires, and
none integrate an AI reasoning layer that personalises the next recommended topic and mission
for each student based on their actual performance profile.
